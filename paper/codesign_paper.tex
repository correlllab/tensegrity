\begin{abstract}
This paper presents a simulation-based design study of a full-size
humanoid whose structure is a prestressed tensegrity network of struts and
cables rather than a rigid skeleton. It addresses which joints of such a
machine must remain conventional bearings for the robot to be
controllable. Three simulated machines of identical anthropometry are compared
under receding-horizon iLQG control: a pure tensegrity machine, a fully
hinged 27-DoF tendon-driven machine, and a hybrid. A wrench-closure
analysis shows that a bearing-free cable joint requires both overlapping
cages and counter-wound cable families to resist disturbances. The pure
machine stands when anchored but presents \cgDof{} free degrees of
freedom, for which no controller was attempted. The fully hinged machine
walks, but its tension network increases peak ground reaction in passive
drops relative to the same skeleton without cables, and its cable
transmission cannot deliver the specified joint torques at mid-stride
poses. The hybrid retains bearings at the hips and knees, where torque
demands are large and pose-dependent, and uses tensegrity joints at the
ankles and waist, where impact loads enter the structure. At 1.68\,m and
\hybridMass\,kg, with cables at the specified series-elastic stiffness,
it walks at \headV\,m/s in \headRate{} trials with \hybridJointHw\,kg of
articulation hardware, and a gait developed on a model with $46\times$
softer cables transfers across the 20--168\,kN/m stiffness range without
retuning. Rigidizing the tensegrity joints increases gait speed but
removes the \jointImpactLo--\jointImpactHi\% impact attenuation measured
at the ankle. The results indicate that the concept warrants hardware
development: the machine has a third to two thirds of the mass of size-matched
humanoids and is controllable by MPC.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}
Mass is a primary constraint in humanoid design: it determines actuator
torque and hence motor and battery mass, the energy of unintended
collisions, and the hazard a human-sized machine poses to people nearby.
Conventional humanoids, serial chains of rigid links with actuators at the
joints, concentrate mass distally and couple strength to weight.

Tensegrity structures \cite{fuller1962patent,snelson1965patent} follow a
different principle: struts are held apart by a network of pre-tensioned
cables, every member is loaded axially, and stiffness is governed by
prestress rather than by cross-section. These properties have motivated
tensegrity landers and rolling robots
\cite{sabelhaus2015superball,caluwaerts2014design,shah2022tensegrity} and
tensegrity joints in sub-scale legs and spines
\cite{jung2018flexural,wen2025knee,mortensen2025leg,ali2025spine}, but not to a walking machine of human size, where their advantages
cannot be settled by static analysis.

This paper reports one iteration of a design loop in which
model-predictive control (MPC) is the inner step: a parametric tensegrity
kinematics is generated, its controllability under tension-only actuation
is tested, actuators and members are sized from simulated load traces, and
a receding-horizon iLQG controller
\cite{li2004ilqr,tassa2012synthesis,howell2022mjpc} is used to balance and
walk the machine. We refer to this procedure as \emph{MPC-in-the-loop
design evaluation}; the design space is not searched automatically, and
each structural change is justified by a measured change in the gait. A
\emph{fully hinged} 27-DoF tendon-driven machine walked first, but its
cable transmission fell short of the required torque at
\authShortGaitBom{} of 27 DoF over the gait (Sec.~\ref{sec:authority})
and its cable network increased impact loads (Sec.~\ref{sec:evidence}).
A \emph{pure tensegrity} machine without hinges collapsed until its joints
were redesigned with overlapping cages and counter-wound cable families
(Sec.~\ref{sec:jointcell}), and only this bearing-free joint attenuated
impacts. The \emph{hybrid} combines the two (Sec.~\ref{sec:hybridwalker}),
and a joint-count study over its rigidized variants quantifies the
trade-off (Sec.~\ref{sec:jointcount}).

The purpose of the study is to determine whether the concept justifies
hardware development, i.e., whether a tensegrity humanoid can be realized
at a fraction of conventional mass while remaining controllable by a
predictive planner. Both conditions are met (Table~\ref{tab:compare},
Sec.~\ref{sec:hybridwalker}). The gait was developed with interface
cables at 2.4\,kN/m, approximately \stiffGapSeries$\times$ softer than the
specified \kSeriesMin--\kSeriesMax\,kN/m series-elastic element, because
this is the stiffest cable the planner integrates at a step fast enough
for design iteration (Sec.~\ref{sec:design}). All headline results are
obtained at 40\,kN/m, within the specified range; results that remain at
the \emph{development configuration} are labeled as such, and the
geometric results are independent of cable stiffness.

The contributions are: (i) a 1.68\,m, \hybridMass\,kg hybrid humanoid,
specified to tube, rope and drive class, that walks at \headV\,m/s in
\headRate{} trials under receding-horizon iLQG at the specified cable
stiffness, compared with 30--80\,kg for built machines of similar stature
(Table~\ref{tab:compare}); (ii) a bearing-free joint between stacked prism cages and the rule that
classical theory implies for it: overlap provides force closure, but only
a counter-wound cable pair provides the strict self-stress
\cite{calladine1978tensegrity}, equivalently wrench closure
\cite{bosscher2004wrench,friesen2018joint}, that tension-only actuators
need to reject disturbances; (iii) a pose-dependent authority bound, the
per-DoF form of the wrench-feasible workspace \cite{gouttefarde2007wfw},
applied as a design gate over the whole gait; and (iv) a joint-count study over rigidized variants of one machine,
yielding measured trade-offs and an allocation rule. To our knowledge, no
prior work compares bearings against cable interfaces within one legged
machine. The MPC architecture and the pipeline that regenerates every
reported quantity from logged runs are supporting infrastructure.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Tensegrity robotics.} Robotic tensegrity
\cite{fuller1962patent,snelson1965patent,skelton2009tensegrity} has been
demonstrated primarily in rolling machines such as SUPERball
\cite{sabelhaus2015superball,caluwaerts2014design}; see
\cite{shah2022tensegrity} for a survey. The closest control precedent is
MPC with inverse-statics tension distribution on tensegrity spines
\cite{sabelhaus2018spinempc}, on low-DoF, quasi-statically loaded modules.
The ULTRA Spine \cite{sabelhaus2015ultra} and Laika \cite{sabelhaus2018laika}
place a tensegrity spine between rigid legs; the present design instead
places the tensegrity interfaces at the joints through which ground
impacts enter. Bearing-free tensegrity joints have been built at limb scale: elbows
and shoulders \cite{lessard2016manipulator}, a 3-DoF joint with wrench
closure enforced at every equilibrium \cite{friesen2018joint}, knee- and
hip-inspired flexural joints \cite{jung2018flexural}, a one-third-scale
rigid--tensegrity biped knee demonstrated in hardware \cite{wen2025knee},
a leg whose tensegrity joints reduce impacts by about one third
\cite{mortensen2025leg}, and variable-stiffness and humanoid spines
\cite{zappetti2020spine,ali2025spine}. None addresses which joints of a
complete humanoid should be tensegrity and which bearings. Classical results (strict self-stress \cite{calladine1978tensegrity},
the elastic--geometric stiffness split \cite{guest2006stiffness},
form-finding \cite{tibert2003form}) are applied without extension.

\textbf{Tendon-driven and compliant humanoids.} Kenshiro \cite{nakanishi2013kenshiro} and Kengoro
\cite{asano2017kengoro} route about one hundred muscles over a
human-mimetic skeleton under the same unilateral tension constraint; here
the tendons constitute the structure itself. Series-elastic actuation
\cite{pratt1995series,tsagarakis2013coman} reappears as a requirement on
the tension members, and soft exosuits \cite{quinlivan2017exosuit} show
that textile load paths carry such forces, motivating an apparel-mounted
structure as a longer-term goal.

\textbf{Co-design and MPC.} Morphology and control have been optimized
jointly by optimal control \cite{ha2018codesign} and reinforcement learning
\cite{schaff2019jointly}; the present work reports one manual iteration
with a predictive controller as the inner step. iLQG synthesizes whole-body humanoid behavior
\cite{tassa2012synthesis,tassa2014control}, whole-body MPC has run on
torque-controlled humanoids \cite{koenemann2015whole,dantec2022whole}, and
MuJoCo MPC \cite{howell2022mjpc} provides the planners used here. The gait
reference follows Raibert \cite{raibert1986legged} and the capture point
\cite{pratt2006capture}; torque budgets follow Winter
\cite{winter2009biomechanics}.

factor; the lightest built machines are tendon-driven with soft shells.
The capability gap is equally significant: GR-1 walks at 1.4\,m/s, whereas

of this study, which asks only whether the structure can be controlled at

\section{The Hybrid Design}
\label{sec:design}
The final design (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}) is 1.68\,m from sole to crown
and \hybridMass\,kg, with 0.40\,m thigh and shank segments (Winter
anthropometry \cite{winter2009biomechanics}). \emph{The hips and knees are
bearings}, because their torque limits, 40--80\,N\,m, are the largest in the
machine and are exercised through large excursions: each knee is an alloy-steel clevis pin in a 61802-class bearing
pair (\hybridKneeHw\,g) and each hip a three-axis gimbal (\hybridHipHw\,g),
sized at a safety factor of \kneeBearingSF{} against the measured
\grfStumble\,N stumble reaction. Articulation hardware at the hips and
knees totals \hybridJointHw\,kg, and the arm hinges add \mbShoulderhw\,kg
of shoulder gimbal hardware. AK70-class drives on the girdle and thigh
cuffs actuate these hinges through tendon loops at a reduction of
approximately 3:1 (12\,mm spool, $\sim$37\,mm joint radius), as in the
hinged machine. \emph{The ankles and waist are tensegrity joints.} Each
foot is a tri-pad plate (heel and two forefoot corners, following the
insertion geometry of the biological ankle \cite{scarr2014biotensegrity})
whose mast overlaps into the shank cage, and the torso is a free body
whose cage nests into the pelvis girdle. Both are held only by the
counter-wound cable interfaces of Fig.~\ref{fig:joint}, with twelve cables
each, no bearing, and force and wrench closure by construction. Because
these cables act at the cage radius rather than through a $\sim$37\,mm
moment arm, the same moment requires four to five times less tension, and
0.165\,kg XM540-class drives replace 0.96\,kg units. Each arm has seven DoF (shoulder gimbal, elbow, three-axis wrist) with
all drives modeled; during walking only the two swing DoF per arm are
planned, the others holding position on servo stiffness. The neck is rigid,
and two 2.5\,kg battery packs are carried on posterior pelvis rails.

Every segment is a Snelson three-strut prism at its form-found
equilibrium twist: released free-floating in zero gravity, the cell
converges to $29.8^\circ\pm0.4^\circ$, consistent with the theoretical
$30^\circ$ \cite{tibert2003form}. Two earlier variants of the same
anthropometry bound the design space: the \emph{fully hinged} 27-DoF
machine, in which every joint is a tendon-driven bearing, and the
\emph{pure tensegrity} machine without bearings
(Sec.~\ref{sec:jointcell}). Joint torque limits follow human walking moments scaled to robot mass
at a safety factor of about 2 (80\,N\,m at hip pitch and knee, 70\,N\,m
at the ankle, 30\,N\,m at the hinged shoulder, 15\,N\,m XM540-class on the
hybrid).

\textbf{Member specification.} Each member is dimensioned from the
simulated load cases rather than covered by a bulk allowance
(Table~\ref{tab:mass}). The \numStruts{} CFRP tubes (12--20\,mm outer diameter) are governed by
the ground reaction entering a cage through its joint
(\strutLoadStumble\,N per strut in a 0.1\,m stumble); Euler sizing at a
safety factor of 2 nevertheless places every strut at the minimum
manufacturable wall (\strutWall\,mm, worst margin
$\strutWorstMargin\times$), so the compression network
(\strutTubeMass\,kg of tube, \strutFittingMass\,kg of fittings) is limited
by manufacturability rather than load. Tendons are 12-strand UHMWPE rope of
1--4\,mm diameter, sized from the larger of the gait's 99th-percentile
tension and $\tau_{\max}/r_{\mathrm{eff}}$ (worst RMS utilization
\ropeUtilRms\%). The rope has a mass of \ropeMass\,kg, whereas its
\numCableEnds{} terminations have a mass of \termMass\,kg and the
\nodeCount{} node clusters \nodeMass\,kg; the mass of such a network is
set by its number of interfaces rather than its number of cables.

\textbf{Cable stiffness: model versus hardware.} The specified rope has a
stiffness of \kRopeMin--\kRopeMax\,MN/m. A deliberate series-elastic
element of \kSeriesMin--\kSeriesMax\,kN/m \cite{pratt1995series} is
required so that prestress can be set at assembly, and the transmission
analysis assumes it. The hybrid was developed with interface cables at
2.4\,kN/m, the \emph{development configuration}. This value was chosen for the planner rather than the hardware: it is
the stiffest interface that the 8\,ms planner step integrates stably (mode
frequency times step 1.84, against a limit of 2), and at this step the
planner runs approximately $8\times$ faster than at the design point
(\hybridRt{} versus $\headRt\times$ real time), which made the tuning runs of the design iteration tractable. Once the design was fixed, the walker was rerun with smaller steps at
the 40\,kN/m design point and across the specified range
(Table~\ref{tab:stiffwalk}); the joint testbed of Sec.~\ref{sec:jointcell}
is also simulated at 40\,kN/m. The hinged
machine's experiments use \kModelMin--\kModelMax\,N/m, with the impact and
stiffness experiments of Sec.~\ref{sec:evidence} rerun at physical
stiffness.

\section{Modeling, Authority, and Control}
\label{sec:authority}
The machines are modeled in MuJoCo \cite{todorov2012mujoco} by a
parametric generator that produces, from a single description of the node
geometry, a \emph{joint-torque} model (motors at the hinges; the planner's
model) and a \emph{cable-driven} model (\numActuatable{} tension-only
actuators). Cables must be tension-only: a MuJoCo tendon with a single
spring length exerts a compressive force when shortened, so all cables use
a deadband spring $F=k\max(0,\ell-\ell_0)$ with $\ell_0$ set by the
prestress fraction and recomputed from the compiled model. Because a cable helixed around a limb has a moment arm of only
16--25\,mm about the limb axis, the yaw DoF carry coaxial capstan drums of
35--55\,mm diameter.

\textbf{Cable authority.} With tension-only actuation, the achievable
torque about DoF $j$ is bounded by
\begin{equation}
  \tau_j^{+} = \sum_c \max(0, M_{jc})\,\Fmax_c,\qquad
  \tau_j^{-} = \sum_c \min(0, M_{jc})\,\Fmax_c,
  \label{eq:authority}
\end{equation}
where $M_{jc}=\partial\ell_c/\partial q_j$ is the moment arm of cable $c$
about joint $j$ and $\Fmax_c$ its tension limit. This is the per-DoF
projection of the wrench-feasibility condition for cable-driven parallel
robots \cite{bosscher2004wrench,gouttefarde2007wfw}; no novelty is claimed
for the bound, only for its application as a requirement over the entire
trajectory. Applied in this way, the bound identified three geometric defects in an
early iteration of the design, among them zero authority in one direction
at the hip yaw and wrist, because all cable families shared one handedness
(Sec.~\ref{sec:jointcell}). Evaluated at the assembly pose alone, the bound
overstates the available authority (Table~\ref{tab:authority}):
at assembly all 27 DoF are bidirectional (worst margin
$\authHomeAll\times$), whereas at logged gait poses \authShortGaitAll{} of
27 DoF fall below their torque limit (hip pitch $\authGaitHip\times$, knee
$\authGaitKnee\times$), and the \numDrivenCables{}-cable,
\numMotors-motor bill of materials leaves \authShortGaitBom{} of 27 short.
The exact zeros in Table~\ref{tab:authority} correspond to capstan drums
whose wrap disengages at certain wrist and shoulder angles. Delivering
80\,N\,m at the leg cables' 1.5\,kN limit requires
$r_{\mathrm{eff}}\ge\rNeedHip$\,mm, whereas the cage provides
\rHaveHip\,mm at hip pitch. This shortfall is the quantitative reason for
retaining bearings at the hips and knees; a second cable of the same
family loses its moment arm at the same poses.

\textbf{Predictive control.} The controller is the asynchronous iLQG
planner of MJPC \cite{howell2022mjpc,tassa2012synthesis} with control
limits \cite{tassa2014control}; the horizon and planning step are 0.6\,s
and 20\,ms for the hinged task and 0.42\,s and 8\,ms for the hybrid (set
by the ankle-cable foot mode), with the plant integrated at 4\,ms.
Rather than shaping the cost by hand, which yields stepping in place, the
controller tracks an \emph{analytic gait reference evaluated inside the
cost}: gait phase
is a function of time alone, the pelvis tracks a lateral sway that unloads
each foot before it lifts, the swing feet follow a sinusoidal clearance
profile, and the landing point is biased by the center-of-mass velocity
error following Raibert \cite{raibert1986legged} and the capture point
\cite{pratt2006capture}; flat-foot inverse kinematics converts foot
targets into joint references. A trial counts as a walk if the pelvis remains above 0.65\,m and the
robot travels more than 1\,m between 3 and 15\,s (the hinged machine's
suite uses pelvis height above 0.55\,m only); upright but stalled trials
are reported separately.

\textbf{Walking results, fully hinged variant.} This machine validates the
pipeline and provides the load traces used for dimensioning: it walks at
\walkSpeed$\pm$\walkSpeedSd\,m/s in \walkRate{} of ten trials, with a longest run of \longRunM\,m in \longRunS\,s, at
\realtimeFactor$\times$ real time. Drives are dimensioned from the tension traces and
the push-recovery bandwidth requirement
($f_{\mathrm{bw}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{k_{\mathrm{ser}}/m_{\mathrm{eff}}}$,
$m_{\mathrm{eff}}=I_rG^2/r^2$), yielding spools where stroke dominates
(hips, knees, waist) and lever arms where bandwidth dominates (ankles,
46\,Hz). This analysis also exposes a limitation of the fully hinged variant: its
\numMotors-motor bill of materials cannot meet the torque specification,
since a single loop delivers \singleLoopHip\,N\,m at hip pitch against a
requirement of 80\,N\,m, and Table~\ref{tab:authority} shows the shortfall
increasing over the gait. Because the limiting quantity is the moment arm
$r_{\mathrm{eff}}$ rather than the number of motors, the hybrid addresses
it by retaining bearings at the hips and knees (Sec.~\ref{sec:design})
rather than by adding actuators.

\section{Evaluation of the Fully Hinged Variant}
\label{sec:evidence}
Five experiments were performed on the fully hinged variant. E1 and E2
compare it against a \emph{rigid-equivalent baseline}, the same 27-DoF
model with the tension network removed and actuation held constant; E3--E5
examine the cable network itself.

\textbf{E1, impact.} In matched passive drops from 0.05--1\,m, the tension
network increases peak ground reaction at every height, by
+\eOneDeltaPassiveStumble\% at 0.1\,m and by
\physOneLimpTen--\physOneLimpHundred\% at physical cable stiffness: a
bearing places the elastic network in parallel with a stiff load path,
which carries the impulse. \textbf{E2, stiffness.} Under a \stiffProbe\,mN differential endpoint probe, cables raise
lateral stiffness by $\stiffCablesRatio\times$, but sweeping assembly
prestress from $0$ to $5\times$ nominal changes it only within
\stiffPreLo--\stiffPreHi\,N/m (by \physTwoEngaged\% at physical stiffness
once slack is taken up), whereas shoulder co-contraction changes it from
\stiffCoLo{} to \stiffCoHi\,N/m ($\stiffCoRatio\times$) without additional
hardware.
\textbf{E3, degradation.} All \eThreeSinglesOk/\eThreeSinglesN{} tested single-cable cuts and all
random losses of up to $k=8$ cables survive standing plus a push; only an adversary selecting against the authority margin causes a fall
at $k=8$.
No tested single cable is structurally critical, whereas one failed
gearbox disables a joint of a rigid robot. \textbf{E4, proximal mass.} Relocating the motor masses to the
joints they drive, at constant total mass, reduces speed from \walkSpeed{}
to \distalSpeed\,m/s (success rate \walkRate{} versus \distalRate, within
sampling uncertainty). \textbf{E5, tendon realizability.} When the logged
walking torques are mapped through the bounded tension distribution, a
cable is pinned at its 1.5\,kN limit in \tensSatPct\% of states, leaving
up to \tensResid\,N\,m undelivered, and the 99th-percentile demand is
\tensNeeded\,N, $1.6\times$ the rating. Re-planning within the achievable envelope, a gait exists for the full
cable network (\eSixtendonallRate) but not for the \numMotors-motor bill
of materials (\eSixtendonbomRate), which isolates the shortfall to the
hinged variant's actuation set rather than to its cable topology.

\section{The Bearing-Free Joint and the Pure Tensegrity Variant}
\label{sec:jointcell}
Whether the hinged variant's lack of impact tolerance and
prestress-tunable stiffness is a property of tensegrity or of its hinges
is settled by an audit of its joints: every articulated interface
constrains all three relative translations, and the wrench-closure linear
program (closure holds if and only if
$\max\{t: W\lambda=0,\ \lambda\ge t\mathbf{1}\}>0$ for the matrix $W$ of
unit-tension cable wrenches \cite{bosscher2004wrench,gouttefarde2007wfw})
finds \wcThree/\wcTotal{} joints able to resist even pure forces by cable
action alone, since every crossing cable pulls the segments together.
Without hinges the robot telescopes and collapses, the pelvis falling
below $0.6\times$ its home height within \collapseT\,s.

\textbf{Force closure requires overlap; wrench closure requires
counter-winding.} In a tensegrity tower, successive stages overlap: the
distal ring sits inside the proximal cage, giving the interface both a
downward and an upward cable family (Fig.~\ref{fig:joint}). On an isolated
two-cage testbed (fixed proximal cage, three floating distal struts, 1\,kg
payload), force closure appears at an overlap of $0.1H$, and from
\jointOverlapFrac$H$ the joint also carries its design load. Force closure alone is insufficient: a margin $t_6=0$ means that the
cable set has no strict self-stress, the classical existence condition for
a tensegrity \cite{calladine1978tensegrity}, hence no co-contraction state
and no moment authority about some axis, so that tension-only actuators
cannot oppose a disturbance about that axis. A controllable joint requires
$t_6>0$. A single cross family wound in one sense cannot provide this;
adding the counter-wound mirror family closes the wrench set at the same
cable count, which is the general form of the opposite-handedness rule
found for the yaw drums and the reason Snelson's towers alternate the
handedness of successive stages.
With the counter-wound pair, the joint carries its load, admits
self-stress at \cocontractN\,N per cable (the single-handed set admits
none), and a passive chain of three such joints stands with less than
\chainSag\,mm of settling.

\textbf{Impact attenuation at the bearing-free joint.} When the testbed
(cables at the specified series-elastic 40\,kN/m) is dropped against a
mass-matched control whose distal cage is rigid and mounted on a ball
joint, the peak ground reaction falls from \jointImpactPinFive{} to
\jointImpactCableFive\,N at 0.05\,m, from \jointImpactPinTen{} to
\jointImpactCableTen\,N at 0.10\,m, and from \jointImpactPinTwenty{} to
\jointImpactCableTwenty\,N at 0.20\,m, an attenuation of
\jointImpactLo--\jointImpactHi\% at every height, whereas the hinged
humanoid showed an increase (E1). Without a bearing, the elastic network is in series with the load
rather than in parallel with a stiff path \cite{sabelhaus2015superball}.
The attenuation is due to this series compliance and not to the topology
alone: at $10\times$ the cable stiffness (400\,kN/m, approaching bare
rope) the comparison inverts and the cable joint lands \stiffSensTenLo{}
to \stiffSensTenHi\% harder than the pin joint. Geometry and series
elasticity are therefore jointly necessary, consistent with bench-scale
tensegrity legs \cite{mortensen2025leg}.
\textbf{Prestress does not tune stiffness at rope modulus}, for a material reason
that follows from the standard decomposition \cite{guest2006stiffness}:
the tangent stiffness is the sum of an elastic term of order $EA/L$ and a
geometric term of order $T/L$, and prestress modulates stiffness only when
the two are comparable. At the rope modulus of this design, the geometric
term is \jointGeomElasticRope\% of the elastic term; variable stiffness is
therefore an actuation cost (co-contraction, E2) rather than an assembly
parameter.

\textbf{The pure tensegrity machine.} Generating the entire humanoid as a graph of overlapping cells, without
bearings, yields a structure that stands with its foot cells anchored
(\cgStruts{} struts, \cgCables{} cables; unloaded limb chains sag by
approximately \cellChainPer\,mm per cell); the pelvis and torso must be
rigid strut clusters, and a 22\,kg allowance for motors and batteries
requires \cgPreNeeded\,N of prestress per cable. No controller was
attempted for its \cgDof{} free DoF, since the \numActuatable-actuator
cable-driven hinged model already required a planning iteration every
4\,ms to stand. This is a limitation of planner tractability rather than
controllability; every counter-wound joint has $t_6>0$, so the structure is
controllable in principle.

\section{Walking Performance of the Hybrid}
\label{sec:hybridwalker}
The prescription that follows from the preceding sections, bearings where
torque requirements dominate and tensegrity interfaces where compliance
dominates, is the hybrid of Sec.~\ref{sec:design}.

\textbf{Gait synthesis (development configuration, 2.4\,kN/m).} The
controller is the analytic-reference iLQG task with the ankle posture
terms removed. With passive ankle cables every configuration tested falls
within three seconds, since without ankle actuation there is no
center-of-pressure control; welding the feet to the shanks produces
immediate walking (\weldedRate{} at \weldedV\,m/s). Providing the planner
with tension actuators on two of the four ankle cable families (twelve
controls) restores walking at \hybridSpeed\,m/s in \hybridRate{} trials at
a running cost below that of the welded control (\hybridCost{} versus
\weldedCost{} per step), one family lifting the swing foot and the other
pitching the toe. Removing one battery pack (\batteryKg\,kg, asymmetric)
leaves the gait intact (\batteryRate{} at \batteryV\,m/s).

\textbf{Design point and stiffness range.} The design point is the same
walker with every ankle and waist cable at the specified 40\,kN/m, run
with the development controller without retuning. It walks in \headRate{}
trials at \headV\,m/s (Wilson 95\% lower bound \headCiLo\%; every trial
advanced at least \headDistMin\,m in the 12\,s window), with hip and knee
duty of \headLegDuty\%, a 99th-percentile command of
\headLegNmPeak\,N\,m, ankle duty of \headAnkleDuty\% (\headAnklePeak\,N)
and torso excursion within $\pm\headTorsoRP^\circ$, at $\headRt\times$
real time. The walker was also run at 20 and 168\,kN/m, each cable retaining its
start-pose tension (rest lengths recomputed) and damping ratio. The
stiffest cable mode rises from 230 to 664, 939 and 1925\,rad/s, so the
planner and plant steps are reduced from 8 and 4\,ms to keep the product
of mode frequency and step at or below the development value, while the
16\,ms planning period and all other settings are unchanged
(Table~\ref{tab:stiffwalk}); a 0.5\,ms step would exceed the 512-knot
horizon limit of MJPC, truncating the horizon to 0.26\,s, in which case
every trial fell. Across the range the development controller walks
without retuning: \walkerKTwentyRate{} at 20\,kN/m
(\walkerKTwentyUpright{} upright for the full 16\,s) and
\walkerKStiffRate{} at 168\,kN/m, where the nearly rigid interfaces
reduce the speed to \walkerKStiffV\,m/s. Leg-torque demand is unchanged
(99th percentile \walkerKFortyLegPnn--\walkerKTwentyLegPnn\,N\,m), whereas
ankle duty decreases from \hybridAnkleDuty\% to \walkerKStiffAnkleDuty\%
and torso excursion from $\pm\torsoRP^\circ$ to
$\pm\walkerKStiffTorsoRP^\circ$ between the development configuration
and 168\,kN/m; the development configuration is therefore optimistic with respect to
torso compliance and conservative with respect to planning cost
(\walkerKTwentyRt--\walkerKStiffRt$\times$ real time). \emph{Parameter robustness.} At 20\,kN/m, ten trials with every
interface cable's stiffness drawn from $\pm30\%$ and its prestress from
$\pm20\%$, every body mass from $\pm15\%$, and ground friction from
$\mu\in[0.3,0.9]$ walk in \randRate{} cases at \randV\,m/s, the lowest
friction walked being \randMuMin. Because planner and plant share the
perturbed model, this measures the tolerance of the tuning to parameter
variation rather than robustness to model mismatch.

\textbf{Capability envelope} (Fig.~\ref{fig:sweeps}; development
configuration unless 40\,kN/m is stated). \emph{Speed.} The hybrid has a reliable command band: \hsSpdThirtyRate{} at a
commanded 0.30\,m/s (\hsSpdThirtyV\,m/s achieved), \hsSpdFortyFiveRate{}
at 0.45\,m/s (\hsSpdFortyFiveV), falls in every trial at 0.60\,m/s, and
undertracking below the band. Retuning (cadence 1.1$\to$0.8\,Hz,
swing-height reference 0.24\,m) yields \agilityV\,m/s in \agilityRate{}
trials with \gaitClr\,mm of clearance (Fig.~\ref{fig:sequence}). Speed
tracking is approximately 42\% of the command against 65--70\% for the
hinged machine; the remaining panels use the pre-retuning configuration. \emph{Trunk payload.} Boxes on the torso are carried above
the tensegrity waist, so that every added kilogram loads the twelve waist
cables. The success rate is \hsPayTwoRate{} at 2\,kg, \hsPayFiveRate{} at
5\,kg, \hsPayEightRate{} at 8\,kg and \hsPayTwelveRate{} at 12\,kg, against
an unaffected rigid trunk up to 20\,kg; at 12\,kg every trial pitches
backward within seven seconds, and shifting the load 4\,cm forward of the
waist axis at a 0.40\,m/s command restores 10\,kg to \payTenRate{}, so the
constraint is dynamic pitch balance rather than static capacity. At
40\,kN/m ($n=3$ per mass) the ceiling is unchanged (\stiffPayTwelveRate{}
at 12\,kg), 2\,kg walks \stiffPayTwoRate, and 5 and 8\,kg fall late
(\stiffPayFiveRate, \stiffPayEightRate) at 0.28--0.41\,m/s, consistent
with the stiffer walker's closer tracking of the speed command; reducing the
command to 0.30\,m/s restores 5\,kg to \stiffPayFiveRetuneRate. Payload
capacity is therefore set by the controller tuning rather than by cable
stiffness.
\emph{Arm carry.} Under a common protocol the hybrid holds \carryHybTwelve\,kg at a 12\,cm
drop threshold against \carryHinTwelve\,kg for the hinged machine,
sag-limited versus motor-limited. At 40\,kN/m the hybrid's sag decreases to
\carryStiffSlope\,mm/kg, it holds \carryStiffTwelve\,kg, and it too
becomes motor-limited (15\,N\,m shoulder saturating at
\carryStiffSatMass\,kg); the development configuration's carry deficit is
thus due to chain compliance rather than drive class.

\textbf{Bearings versus cable interfaces.} A bearing delivers full torque
at every joint angle, whereas the authority of a cable joint collapses
mid-stride (Table~\ref{tab:authority}), and each bearing removes three to
five DoF from the planner's state (46 for the hybrid, \cgDof{} for the
pure machine). A cable interface, in turn, provides impact attenuation ($-27$..$32\%$ at the ankle
testbed), torso compliance ($\pm\headTorsoRP^\circ$ at the design point,
$\pm\torsoRP^\circ$ in the development configuration), lighter actuation
(0.165 versus 0.96\,kg drives), graceful degradation, and robustness to
mass changes (battery-removal test). Its costs are reduced \emph{speed} (rigidizing increases gait speed
monotonically, $\hybridSpeed\rightarrow\jcRAnkleV\rightarrow\jcRBothV$\,m/s
under one controller in the development configuration), increased
\emph{planner load} ($\headRt\times$ against $\realtimeFactor\times$ real
time), reduced \emph{payload} (Fig.~\ref{fig:sweeps}b), and \emph{tuning
sensitivity}: the gait tuned for the compliant waist does not walk (\jcRWaistRate)
when that joint is rigidized. Success rates along the axis
are indistinguishable at these sample sizes; the speed ordering is the
measured effect.

\textbf{Allocation rule.} Bearings should be placed where the gait's torque requirements
concentrate, and series-elastic cable interfaces where impacts enter the
structure. The hips and knees carry the largest, most pose-dependent
torques and no first impact; the ankles contact the ground first and require substantially less
moment (roughly half at the 99th percentile); the waist is the marginal case,
providing torso compliance at a cost in speed, payload and tuning. The rule reflects present-day drives and planners rather than a
fundamental limit.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{Limitations.} All results are obtained in simulation, without
tendon friction, hysteresis, creep or cable mass. The headline walker,
payload, carry, robustness and joint drop tests are evaluated at the
specified 20--168\,kN/m; the speed sweep, joint-count study and ankle
controls remain at the 2.4\,kN/m development configuration. In hardware, tendon friction and hysteresis will bias the delivered
tension and cause prestress to drift; the series-elastic element mitigates
both by converting tension into a displacement measurement, but tension
sensing and periodic re-tensioning must be provided for, while cable mass
(\ropeMass\,kg) is negligible. The planner runs at $\headRt\times$ real
time at the design point; a shorter horizon on a reduced model, a
multi-rate scheme with a fast tension loop, or a policy distilled from the
planner's rollouts would be required to close this gap, none of which is
evaluated here. The AK70-10-class hip
and knee drives (24.8\,N\,m peak, approximately 8\,N\,m rated) deliver
approximately 77\,N\,m peak and 26\,N\,m continuous through the 3:1 tendon
reduction; the planner's 80\,N\,m bound corresponds to the peak, the mean
command (\headLegDuty\% duty) lies within the continuous rating, and the
99th percentile (\headLegNmPeak\,N\,m) within the peak. Over
\headTorqueWalkSeconds\,s of logged walking at the design point, commands
above the 74\,N\,m drive peak occur in \headTorqueOverPeakEpisodes{}
isolated episodes of at most \headTorqueOverPeakMaxMs\,ms, commands above
the continuous rating occupy \headTorqueOverContPct\% of the time, and the
RMS demand, which determines thermal load, is \headMotorRmsNm\,N\,m at the
motor for the worst joint, \headMotorRmsPct\% of the rating. The sample sizes ($n=3$--10) resolve speed differences but not
success-rate differences of one or two trials.

\section{Conclusion}
Within a single design family, a humanoid composed only of tensegrity
cells stands when anchored, given overlapping cages and counter-wound
cable families, but presents \cgDof{} free DoF for which no controller is
available; a fully hinged variant walks, but its cables increase impact
loads and cannot deliver the specified torques at gait poses. Between
these extremes, a 1.68\,m, \hybridMass\,kg hybrid with bearings at the
eight torque-carrying DoF and tensegrity joints where impacts enter walks
at \headV\,m/s in \headRate{} trials at the specified cable stiffness,
with a gait that transfers across the entire 20--168\,kN/m range.
Rigidizing its tensegrity joints increases speed and removes compliance;
removing its bearings removes planner tractability. As a design study, the
results indicate that the concept warrants hardware development: the
machine has a third to two thirds of the mass of size-matched humanoids
and is controllable by a predictive planner. The allocation rule and trade-off
data constitute one iteration of an MPC-in-the-loop design process and
identify the parameters for the next, namely moment-arm geometry and cable
stiffness.
